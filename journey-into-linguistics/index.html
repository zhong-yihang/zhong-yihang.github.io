My academic journey has been shaped by a persistent desire to explore the universal principles and parametric variations underlying natural languages, a shift that began when I transferred from education to linguistics during my undergraduate studies. While education studies offered valuable insights into how policies, values, and practices vary across contexts, I found myself drawn to the pursuit of systematic, universal laws, especially those governing human language.

A pivotal moment in my passion for going deeper into linguistics occurred during an informal discussion with a tutor in my previous B.A. course on Translation: Practice & Theory (Linguistics). After my presentation, he remarked: “Jakobson and Halliday’s research on structural linguistics demonstrates their identity as 70% linguist and 30% semiotician.” This comment reframed my perspective, highlighting how even scholars renowned for their linguistic contributions were not necessarily the representatives of different linguistic schools but extended their inquiries into interdisciplinary realms. The interesting chat prompted me to view interdisciplinarity not as a divergence from linguistics but as a means to enrich and expand the field scope. Inspired by this revelation, I began seeking ways to integrate linguistics with neighboring disciplines.

At The Chinese University of Hong Kong (CUHK), I entered M.A. in Applied Linguistics, but I quickly found myself unsettled by how the field often defined “application”, as if application meant leaving the linguistic core behind and attaching oneself to pedagogy, clinical settings, or policy. That never sat right with me. To me, genuine application must ontologically grow from linguistics, not depart from it.

What the public record of my publications does not show is the quiet, humiliating arc of four failed PhD application cycles, each one an education in how the world actually works. The first cycle, 2022-2023, was barely serious: fresh into my M.A., I threw applications to CUHK PhD linguistics and applied linguistics programs, knowing even then I had bare chance. I was testing waters, not crossing them. The second cycle, 2023-2024, was different: I had one SSCI publication, but my research identity was still a fog. I applied only to elite programs, yet I could not have told my clear line of research inquiry. No committee could possibly have extracted coherence from my scattered interests. The rejections were deserved, if painful.

The third cycle, 2024-2025, was when things changed. I had genuine strength: multiple publications, experimental competence, a mature sense of what questions drove me. However, Trump took office, and the funding was cut down. PhD slots shrank mid-cycle. I received waitlists at Ohio State University and Connecticut University, and then rejections. I was finally ready, but the door was narrowing.

The fourth cycle, just past, was my strongest by every metric. My thinking had crystallized; I could defend why my range (from L1 to L2, from children to adults, from behavioral to neuro methods) was not chaotic but coherent around the s-side (syntax/semantics/pragmatics) stance. The published record speaks for itself. Yet the number of funded linguistics PhD slots in America fell even further. I made the waitlist at Northwestern University and UC Santa Cruz. Both ended in no. I learnt from an intended supervisor that I was extremely controversial as my interests are too broad for a department that now needs exceptional fit. Many faculty members even look for specific linguistic phenomenon to supervise. The Stanford student who took the slot even didn't have any offer elsewhere. That is the climate now.

After four cycles, I have learned that admissions is not a meritocracy of ideas. It is a brutal matching game played with shrinking chips, where institutional pedigree, recommendation letter rhetoric, and political funding shifts matter as much as intellectual depth. I am still here, not because I am stubborn, though I am, but because many linguistically-oriented questions remain yet to be answered.
