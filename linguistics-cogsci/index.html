How I View Linguistics & Cognitive Science

I have long been preoccupied with the relationship between linguistics and cognitive science, a tension that has shaped my research identity, my reflections on disciplinary boundaries, and my understanding of what constitutes meaningful, enduring inquiry. What began as technical curiosity has evolved into a sustained philosophical inquiry into the nature of explanation, the politics of academic value, and the fragile bridge between specialized theoretical depth and broad interdisciplinary impact.

My perspective crystallized during a research group meeting in 2025, when I presented a study (not my own) linking syntactic movement to neural activation in Broca’s area. I chose the paper precisely because it integrated formal syntactic theory with neuroscientific evidence, a combination I still consider valuable. After my presentation, a senior collaborator with a strong background in psychology and neuroscience challenged the broader contribution: was the study merely mapping linguistic structures onto brain regions, or did it uncover general principles of cognition? She drew a sharp line between linguistically oriented and psychologically oriented research, arguing that high‑impact journals prioritize questions that resonate across disciplines, not just within a narrow theoretical community. She noted that work confined to linguistic mechanisms often struggles for visibility, funding, and high citation metrics, whereas research framed around general cognitive architecture, like memory, attention, and inhibitory control, attracts wider attention and resources. I pushed back gently, defending the intrinsic value of domain‑specific inquiry, the legitimacy of curiosity‑driven research, and the danger of reducing quality to journal prestige or impact factor. Yet her words stayed with me, forcing me to confront a deeper tension: between the integrity of linguistic theory and the pressure to reframe linguistic questions as windows onto general cognition.

The linguistics stance & the psychology stance
Over time, I have come to see this tension as a defining feature of my intellectual identity. I now distinguish two orientations that often overlap but remain logically distinct: the linguistic stance, which uses experimental methods to understand linguistic structure and its associated mental representation; and the psychological stance, which uses language as a tool to understand general cognitive architecture. The linguistic stance asks: how does the mind compute language? It treats language as a privileged window into uniquely human computational capacities, with its own internal logic, constraints, and developmental timing. The psychological stance asks: how does language reveal general cognition? It treats language as one manifestation of broader systems. In practice, many of my projects embody the first orientation: I begin with a theoretically significant linguistic phenomenon, such as telicity, pronoun resolution, counterfactual conditionals, genitive relative construction, and pseudo‑passive morphology, followed by designing experiments to test how these structures are processed and acquired. My goal is not to reduce language to non‑linguistic functions, but to show how linguistic representations interact with cognitive systems without being exhausted by them.

This positioning has shaped my experience with academic publishing and peer review, revealing deep disciplinary biases in how “importance” is judged. Manuscripts rooted in theoretical linguistics are often criticized in psychology journals as too specialized, too technical, or insufficiently focused on general mechanisms. Reviewers and editors frequently ask me to downplay linguistic theory, prioritize broader cognitive conclusions, and frame effects as instances of more general principles such as similarity‑based interference. I understand this expectation: cognitive science as a field has organized itself around a set of shared problems that cut across domains, and work that can’t be translated into this shared language faces barriers to dissemination. Yet I also resist the implication that linguistically deep research is merely narrow or niche. Linguistics addresses uniquely human questions: how do we compose infinite meanings from finite elements? How do children acquire abstract structural knowledge from limited input? How do crosslinguistic patterns reflect both biological constraints and historical contingency? These are not trivial puzzles; they are foundational to what it means to be human.

My reflections have expanded beyond individual projects to the political economy of knowledge: why some disciplines attract more funding, attention, and prestige than others, even when their intellectual rigor and intrinsic interest are comparable. I have discussed this with peer researchers, asking why theoretical mathematics enjoys stable support while theoretical linguistics struggles for resources, why psychology and neuroscience dominate the cognitive science landscape, and why work framed as “about the brain” often carries more weight than work framed as “about language”. The answer, I have concluded, is not purely scientific but instrumental and narrative. Disciplines thrive when they can tell a compelling story about their social value: mathematics supports national security, technology, and finance; physics promises cosmic insight; neuroscience connects to health, education, and AI. Linguistics, especially formal theoretical linguistics, has struggled to articulate a similarly compelling public narrative. Its contributions are real but often long‑term, indirect, and abstract. Applications such as language teaching, translation technology, or computational linguistics are visible, but they are often disconnected from the core theoretical questions that drive my work. As a result, linguistics remains peripherally positioned within cognitive science, even though language is arguably the most distinctive expression of human cognition.

A commitment, not a compromise
My commitment to a linguistically grounded cognitive science has only strengthened. I reject any choice between linguistic integrity and interdisciplinary impact, instead practicing experimental linguistics that is theoretically responsible, methodologically rigorous, and philosophically reflective. My view of linguistics and cognitive science is integrative, not reductive: language is a privileged part of cognition, revealing unique computational capacities. The mind contains specialized systems that interact dynamically, and my goal is to understand both the specializations and the interactions regarding how linguistic computation operates, develops, varies crosslinguistically, and engages neural systems. I refuse to frame every conclusion as an instance of a broader cognitive principle, just as I refuse to believe that interdisciplinary relevance demands abandoning theoretical depth. Enduring contributions come from those who know their home discipline deeply while speaking across boundaries with clarity. For me, that means remaining a theoretically committed experimental linguist who sharpens linguistic questions rather than diluting them. Language is not just a tool for communication; it is a royal road to the human mind.
