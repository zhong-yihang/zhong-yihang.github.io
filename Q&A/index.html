Q: What do you study and what don’t you study?

A: My core inquiry lies in experimental linguistics conceived in a broad sense, covering formal linguistic foundations, first and second language acquisition, developmental patterns from childhood to adulthood, and real‑time sentence comprehension and production across populations. Methodologically, I integrate behavioral methods (including but definitely not limited to acceptability judgment, self‑paced reading, elicited production) and neural imaging methods (e.g. EEG, fMRI) to probe mental representations and cognitive mechanisms, while remaining theoretically anchored in syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and their interfaces. Despite this wide scope, my research is consistently guided by a hypothetico‑deductive, falsifiability‑oriented scientific logic, with tightly coupled theoretical analysis and experimental design that prioritizes precise structural control and epistemic rigor. This broad‑based, multi‑method, multi‑population framework serves a unified agenda: to uncover the computational design, constraints, and developmental trajectory of the language faculty as part of human cognitive architecture.

I do not pursue research that drifts from linguistic phenomena, structural reality, or any ontological foundation of language. Accordingly, I stand against some versions of “applied linguistics” that abandon the study of language structure and internal mechanisms, instead reducing inquiry to external social, educational, or policy‑oriented problem‑solving WITHOUT epistemic grounding in linguistic theory. I also do not engage in purely atheoretical, data‑driven description, surface‑level interdisciplinarity, or disciplinary “problem‑feeding” that erodes the core identity and explanatory coherence of linguistics. I do not treat language as a neutral tool to investigate general cognition alone, nor do I reduce linguistic inquiry to engineering performance or social intervention without commitment to structural explanation. In short, while rejecting any “applied” agenda that loses touch with the empirical and theoretical foundations of language as its central object, my work embodies a methodologically applied, interdisciplinary orientation that translates formal theories and structural insights into testable experiments and empirically grounded cognitive models.

Q: What journals and scholars interest you most?

A: I especially enjoy reading Applied Psycholinguistics, Language Acquisition, Language, and Cognitive Science. They cover the processing field, the acquisition field, the general linguistics field, and the broad spectrum of cognitive science. I hope to contribute my works to these excellent journals.

I admire Jon Sprouse, an experimental linguist. His goal is to map out the hypothesis space of possible combinatorial systems in natural languages that could be implemented in the brain (at some level of abstraction). He is so knowledgeable about various behavioral and neuro research methods and shows methodological transparency on his website!

Q: How do you think of the current status of generative linguistics in relation to science?

A: I argue that generative linguistics can and should retain its theoretical core by adopting a clear domain of Galilean idealization, explicit interface parameters that link the abstract system to performance factors, and a standard of constrained consistency across experiments. This framework justifies idealization as a legitimate scientific strategy, while distinguishing the core computational system from extra-domain cognitive systems such as memory, attention, and inhibitory control. It does not seek isomorphism between formal structures and observable behavior, but requires predictable, coherent patterns across multiple measures.

Meanwhile, I fully understand another influential critique: if generative linguistics claims to study the biological foundation of language, it must abide by the empirical standards of natural and cognitive sciences; if it chooses to remain a pure formal discipline, it should avoid linking itself to biological or psychological mechanisms. From this perspective, any position between the two risks becoming inconsistent and unaccountable, as it can't satisfy the standards of either formal inquiry or empirical science. This view insists that the field must choose a clear identity rather than remaining in an ambiguous middle ground.

I continue to reflect deeply on this tension. Even as I advocate for integrating formal idealization and empirical constraint, many critical questions remain open: how many interface parameters are sufficient to maintain theoretical coherence? How stringent should cross-disciplinary evidence be in evaluating formal models? To what extent should a priori formal reasoning yield to behavioral and neural data? These issues occupy a complex, gray area with no straightforward answers, and I remain committed to exploring them rigorously.

Q: How to become a linguist? What qualities are needed?

A: To become a linguist, you first need a deep, unshakable curiosity about language as a window into the human mind and/or social reality (depending upon your philosophical stance), not just how we speak, but why we speak the way we do. This requires grounding yourself in the core of linguistic theory: phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and their interfaces, to build a precise understanding of natural languages. Equally critical is a commitment to methodological rigor, whether through formal analysis, experimental design, or philosophical inquiry, as the best work bridges theoretical speculation with empirical evidence.

Qualitatively, the most essential trait is intellectual resilience. As my own journey shows, academic paths are rarely linear. Rejections, funding shifts, and disciplinary biases are inevitable, but they refine your focus rather than define your worth. You must also cultivate philosophical clarity: understanding not just how to answer questions, but why they matter, and how to organize your thoughts to broader audiences. Finally, you need the courage to stay true to your intellectual identity, balancing interdisciplinary curiosity with the integrity of your core discipline.

Q: What is your academic aspiration in a long run?

A: Now I just want to be admitted by a PhD program. In the longer term, I plan to establish a journal titled Experimental Syntax/Semantics/Pragmatics and serve as its editor-in-chief. The journal will feature only high-quality studies with original theoretical contributions and rigorous cross-verification between linguistic theories and experimental findings. I aim to secure sponsorship to make it fully open-access, allowing free access for readers and free publication for authors. With sufficient financial support in the future, I also hope to build a linguistics museum to popularize this academic field.

Q: May I collaborate with you?

A: I welcome collaborators who share a theory-driven, hypothesis-testing mindset and a commitment to theoretical clarity, methodological rigor, and replicable empirical work. Ideal partners value linguistic reasoning, understand syntax-semantics-pragmatics representations, and respect the logic of falsifiability, statistical validation, and theory-experiment mutual constraint. They communicate transparently, meet deadlines reliably, take ownership of their role, and aim for cumulative, interpretable findings.

We may not be a nice fit if your work is purely descriptive, seeks so-called application WITHOUT epistemic grounding in linguistic theory, or has little interest in hypothesis testing about linguistic theory and cognitive mechanisms. This includes researchers focusing only on language teaching, classroom pedagogy, translation practices, sociolinguistic surveys, critical discourse analysis, computational modeling without cognitive interpretability, or large‑scale corpus description unconstrained by linguistic theory. Collaboration is also challenging if you prioritize social issues or qualitative interpretation over falsifiable predictions and statistical validation.
