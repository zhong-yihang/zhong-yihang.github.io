As an experimental s-sider, my research focuses on experimental linguistics (both acquisition and processing), grounded in theories of syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and their interfaces (s-side). Ontologically, I appreciate linguistic limits (i.e. how much specific subsystems can vary across languages) and predictors (i.e. what features may co-occur cross-linguistically). Epistemologically, I believe that linguistics is ultimately a part of cognitive science. Hence, I investigate how the language-specific biological faculty operates during language comprehension and production, and how it integrates into the broader context of the mind and brain (i.e. general cognitive faculty). Methodologically, I employ various behavioral and neural methods in experimental science to explore the interaction between linguistic theory and cognitive architecture.

Inspired by the natural sciences, my research in experimental linguistics follows a theory-driven, hypothetico-deductive logic, pursuing falsifiability and statistical validation. Its defining characteristic lies in its focus on implicit mental representations, which must be measured indirectly through experimental tasks, with variable operationalization relying on rigorous grammatical control. The measurement tools in my work are highly theory-laden; experimental design is deeply coupled with theoretical structural analysis, forming a philosophical paradigm of reciprocal validation between theory and experimentation.

A subsidiary thread that runs consistently through my work is the philosophy of language science, which interrogates the epistemological and ontological foundations of linguistic inquiry. For instance, I examine the legitimacy of Galilean idealization in formal linguistics, the falsifiability and resilience of theoretical frameworks, the modal distinction between necessary truth and accidental empirical generalization, and the disciplinary identity of linguistics within the broader architecture of cognitive science. I critically engage with tensions between rationalist and empiricist methodologies, the theoretical challenge posed by large language models, and the criteria that distinguish epistemically grounded research from merely nominal interdisciplinarity. This philosophical strand does not stand apart from my experimental program but rather underpins it. I aim to clarify what counts as genuine explanation, how theoretical constructs interface with experimental evidence, and how linguistics can maintain its disciplinary integrity while contributing meaningfully to the science of the mind.
